# 4. What the notes are called

Everything so far has been about where a note sits and how long it lasts.
Neither of those depends on words. But sooner or later you have to say a note
out loud, and what comes out of your mouth depends on where you learned.

## Seven names, and then they start again

There are only seven, and then the pattern repeats. In Britain, Ireland and
North America they are letters:

:::music-piece{caption="Seven steps up, and the eighth is the first one again"}

```abc
X:1
T:The scale of C
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=110
K:C
C D E F|G A B c|
```

Press play and watch the name change. The eighth note is called :note[C] again
— the same letter as the first, because it sounds like the first one over
again, only higher.

:::

In France, Italy, Spain and much of Latin America, the same seven notes have
names that came from a hymn: the first syllable of each line of *Ut queant
laxis*, an eleventh-century tune to St John. Guido of Arezzo used it to teach
singers, and the syllables stuck.

:::music-piece{names="solfege" caption="The same seven notes, the same order, other words"}

```abc
X:1
T:La gamme de do
M:4/4
L:1/4
Q:1/4=110
K:C
C D E F|G A B c|
```

The tune has not changed — only what the notes are called. It starts on
:note[Do] and climbs through :note[Ré] and :note[Mi] to :note[Sol], and the
eighth note is :note[Do] again, an octave higher.

:::

## The two lists, side by side

| Letters | Syllables |
| --- | --- |
| C | Do (or Ut) |
| D | Ré |
| E | Mi |
| F | Fa |
| G | Sol |
| A | La |
| B | Si |

Two things are worth noticing, and the second one surprises people.

**The lists start in different places.** The letters begin at A, the syllables
at Do — and Do is C, not A. That is not an error anyone can fix now: the letters
were named first, from a scale that started on what we call A, and the
syllables were named later from a tune that started somewhere else.

**Do is always C.** In France or Italy, *do* names a pitch, the way *C* does —
it does not move when the music changes key. There is another system, taught
mostly in English-speaking countries, where *do* means "whichever note the
music is resting on". Both are called solfège and they are not the same thing.
If you ever find two musicians talking past each other about *do*, that is why.

## The one that catches people out

German-speaking countries use letters, which sounds like good news until you
meet the seventh one. They write **H** where everyone else writes B — and they
keep **B** for the note a semitone below it, which everyone else calls B flat.

So a German edition of a piece in B flat major is a piece in *B-Dur*, and the
note written H is the one you would call B. It is a genuine trap when reading
an old score, and it is worth knowing before you meet it rather than after.

## What everybody agrees about

Underneath the words, nothing is in dispute. Every system has the same seven
steps in the same order, repeating at the octave. Every one of them puts those
notes on the same stave in the same places. Someone who reads only letters and
someone who reads only syllables can sit at the same music stand and play the
same piece, and neither needs to know what the other is thinking.

> 📌 **Key concept**: the notes are the same everywhere. Only the names are
> local — and the page, not the name, is what the two of you are both reading.
