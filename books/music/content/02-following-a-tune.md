# 2. Following a tune

Reading a stave one note at a time is slow, and nobody does it for long. What
a musician actually does is *recognise shapes*: a step up, a leap, a turn back
on itself. This chapter is about seeing those shapes, and about a tune you can
point at while you talk about it.

:::music-piece{caption="Ode to Joy, first phrase"}

```abc
X:1
K:C
EEFG|GFED|CCDE|E3D|
```

The phrase starts on :note[E] and goes almost nowhere. Two of the same note,
then a step up, then another — it climbs four notes in four bars and comes back
down again. Touch any of them and the score above will show you where you are.

The climb stops at :note[G], which is as high as this phrase ever gets. That is
the whole shape: a gentle rise, a turn, and a walk home.

Coming down, the tune passes :note[D] and reaches :note[C] — the lowest note
here, and the one the melody has been leaning towards all along. Play it and it
sounds finished, which is not an accident of this tune but a fact about where it
sits in the key.

Then it climbs again, the same way, and stops one note short. The last
:note[D]{nth=3} is the tune refusing to finish, which is why the phrase feels
like a question and the next one like its answer.

:::

## Why the shape is easier than the notes

Nobody reads the phrase above as "E, E, F, G, G, F, E, D". They read it as *two
of those, up, up, then back the way we came* — and the page shows exactly that,
because the notes are drawn where they sound.

> 💡 **Tip**: when a line looks hard, look at its outline first. A run of notes
> that climbs steadily is one idea, however many notes are in it.
